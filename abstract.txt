Multiplexed imaging technologies such as CODEX, IMC, and MIBI-TOF enable simultaneous visualization of dozens of protein markers at single-cell resolution, offering an unprecedented view of the tumor microenvironment. A central challenge is learning compact cell representations useful for cell type classification, clustering, and spatial analysis. Conventional convolutional architectures address this by fusing all channels early, treating the marker dimension analogously to RGB — an inductive bias well-suited for natural images but misaligned with multiplexed imaging, where each protein channel carries independent biological information.

We present a self-supervised framework built around a channel-isolated inductive bias. Our model, trained with contrastive learning on small task-specific datasets, processes each marker independently through depthwise convolutions before allowing selective cross-channel interaction in later stages. Benchmarked across classical Hodgkin lymphoma (CODEX), triple-negative breast cancer (MIBI-TOF), and neuroblastoma (IMC), this approach consistently outperforms early-fusion baselines and matches or exceeds recently published large-scale pretrained foundation models — including KRONOS, pretrained on 47 million image patches — demonstrating that the right inductive bias can substitute for scale.

We further demonstrate practical utility in two ways. Using Integrated Gradients attribution, we show the model assigns biologically meaningful importance to individual markers, enabling unsupervised cell type assignment without labels. By embedding larger tissue patches, we recover spatially coherent neighborhood structures and show that patient-level aggregation captures clinically relevant variation in neuroblastoma tumor microenvironments.

Together, these results argue that domain-appropriate architectural choices — rather than dataset scale — are the primary driver of representation quality in multiplexed imaging, and that such representations are directly actionable for biological discovery.
